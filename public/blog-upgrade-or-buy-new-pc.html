Is my PC worth upgrading, or should I buy new?

A simple checklist for deciding whether to upgrade a gaming PC or replace it - Windows 11, the part that is actually slow, what the rest can take, and the math.

5 min read upgrades buying advice windows

Short answer: if your PC runs Windows 11 and only one part is holding your games back, upgrade that part. Most of the time it is the graphics card, then memory, then the drive. Buy new when the PC cannot move to Windows 11, when three or more parts need replacing, or when the upgrades add up to most of the price of a new build. Here is the checklist we use when someone sends us a photo of the inside of their PC.

1. Can it run Windows 11?

This decides everything else. Microsoft ended support for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025. A Windows 10 PC keeps working, but it no longer gets security fixes, and Microsoft's own page says that leaves it at greater risk from malware. Their consumer Extended Security Updates program covers a Windows 10 PC until October 12, 2027 - time, not a future.

Windows 11 needs a supported processor, a TPM (Trusted Platform Module) version 2.0 and UEFI firmware with Secure Boot. The quick check is Start, Settings, Windows Update: if your PC is eligible, the upgrade is free and Windows offers it there. If Windows says the PC does not meet the requirements, a new graphics card does not fix that, and a new build is the honest answer. Parts that still have value - a good case, a recent drive - can come with you.

2. Which part is actually slow?

Open Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc), play for ten minutes, then check the Performance tab.

  • Graphics card near 100% and frame rates low - the card is the limit. This is the most common case and the upgrade that changes the most.
  • Memory near full while you play, with stutter when you switch to Discord or a browser - add memory. Our 16GB or 32GB post covers how to decide.
  • Processor at 100% while the graphics card sits at 60% - the processor is the limit. Sometimes a faster chip fits the same socket; sometimes it does not, and that is where an upgrade turns into a new PC.
  • Long load times and a slow desktop but fine frame rates in game - the drive. Moving from a hard drive or an older SATA SSD (solid state drive) to an NVMe SSD is a cheap, noticeable fix.
  • Nothing is pegged, but the PC got loud and slow over the years - it needs a clean, not a part. Dust and dried-out thermal paste do exactly this. That is our $79 tune-up, at your home.

3. Can the rest of the PC take the new part?

Before buying a part, check three things:

  • Power supply. A bigger graphics card needs enough wattage and the right power cables. Check the label on the supply and the card's requirements on the maker's spec page.
  • Case clearance. Modern cards are long and thick. Measure from the back of the case to the drive cage or front fans.
  • Memory type. Memory has to match the motherboard. The kits in our builds are DDR5; a DDR4 board needs a DDR4 kit, and a move to DDR5 means a new board and processor too.

A card upgrade that also needs a new power supply is still usually fine. One that needs a new power supply, a new case and more memory is a new PC bought one piece at a time, and it is time to compare against buying new.

When buying new is the right call

  • Windows 11 is not available for the PC.
  • The processor is the bottleneck and a faster one does not fit the board.
  • Three or more parts are due at once.
  • You want every part new, covered by its maker's warranty, with one year of our labor on top, instead of adding new parts to a PC whose other parts may fail next.

Every build we sell uses the AMD AM5 platform with an upgradeable processor socket, so the next time you face this question, the answer is more likely to be "upgrade".

When upgrading is the right call

  • The PC runs Windows 11 and one part shows up as the limit in Task Manager.
  • The power supply and case can take the new part.
  • You like the PC and want to keep it - a tune-up plus one upgrade often does it.
