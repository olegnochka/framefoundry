Gaming PC for streaming: what changes, and what does not

Streaming from a gaming PC needs 32GB of memory, a graphics card with a hardware encoder and enough upload speed. Which of our builds already have all three.

5 min read streaming memory graphics cards buying advice

Short answer: a PC that streams well is a gaming PC with 32GB of memory, a graphics card with a built-in video encoder, and an internet plan with enough upload speed. Every card in our builds has the encoder, because every current Radeon and GeForce card does. The Performance build ($2,699) is the one we describe as the streaming pick: 32GB of memory and a Radeon RX 9070 XT. The Mainstream ($2,099) can stream too, but we would move it to 32GB first, about $230 more at today's memory prices. The processor matters less than you might expect, as long as the card does the encoding.

Let the graphics card do the encoding

Live streaming means the PC renders the game and, at the same time, compresses the video into a stream small enough to send over the internet. That compression, called encoding, is the extra work. There are two ways to do it:

  • Software encoding (x264) uses the processor cores. Twitch's broadcasting guidelines warn that it takes a lot of the processor and lowers your frame rate, and that its better-looking presets need a processor with six or more cores.
  • Hardware encoding uses a small dedicated encoder built into the graphics card. Twitch recommends it because the game keeps its performance while you stream.

Every card in our builds has a hardware encoder, confirmed on the makers' spec pages:

AV1 is the newest format and gives the same picture at a lower bitrate than H.264. YouTube's live encoder settings recommend 12 Mbps for 1080p at 60 frames per second in AV1 or HEVC, against 17 Mbps in H.264. Twitch's published settings are still built around H.264 at 6,000 kbps for 1080p60, so on Twitch the format matters less than having an encoder at all. OBS Studio, the free streaming software most people use, runs on Windows 10 and 11 and has an Auto Configuration Wizard that picks settings for your PC and connection.

Memory: this is where 16GB runs out

A game on its own is fine with 16GB, as we found in our memory post. Streaming adds OBS, a browser tab with your chat, Discord and music, all running next to the game. That is the case where 16GB fills up and Windows starts moving data out to the SSD (solid state drive), which shows up as stutter in the game and dropped frames in the stream.

This is why the Performance build ships with 32GB, and why the Mainstream page says moving to 32GB is the first upgrade we suggest. Memory is expensive right now. Our price log has the Corsair Vengeance 32GB kit at $499.99 on Oct 31 (it was $479.99 when we started logging on Oct 14) and the Kingston FURY 16GB kit at $269.99 (from $259.99). The gap between the two kits, about $230, is the real cost of making the Mainstream build stream-ready. It is still cheaper than finding out on your first stream.

The processor matters less than you expect

With the graphics card encoding, the processor goes back to its normal job: running the game. The Ryzen 5 9600X in the Mainstream and Performance builds has six cores and twelve threads, which also clears Twitch's six-core bar if you ever want to try x264. The Ryzen 7 9800X3D in the Quiet Creator adds two cores and 3D V-Cache (see our X3D post). It is the better chip for someone who also edits the recordings afterwards, which is what that build is for.

Upload speed: check this before you buy anything

The stream leaves your house at the bitrate you set. Twitch's recommended 1080p60 setting is 6,000 kbps, which is 6 Mbps of upload, constant, for the whole stream. Your internet plan's headline number is the download speed; upload is usually much lower, and it is the number that matters here. Run a speed test at the time of day you would stream and look at the upload figure. We suggest at least double your stream bitrate as headroom, because the stream shares the line with the game, voice chat and everyone else at home. If the upload is tight, Twitch's 720p60 setting at 4,500 kbps is the fallback, and a cable to the router gives a steadier upload than Wi-Fi.

A second monitor and the rest of the desk

Most streamers run a second screen for chat and OBS. The cards in our builds have several outputs: NVIDIA lists three DisplayPort and one HDMI on the RTX 5070 Ti, with up to four displays, and AMD lists DisplayPort 2.1a and HDMI 2.1b on both Radeon cards. A second monitor is a cable, not an upgrade. It does not need to be fast or large; a basic 1080p screen is fine. We sell monitors at the same cost plus 5% as everything else, and our in-home setup connects the monitors, the network and the game launchers before we leave. A USB microphone and webcam plug straight into the motherboard.

Which build

  • Streaming now and then, mostly 1080p games: Mainstream with the 32GB memory upgrade (about $230 more).
  • Streaming regularly at 1080p60 while you play at 1440p: Performance, as built.
  • Streaming plus editing the recordings, in a quiet room: Quiet Creator, whose 2TB SSD has room for the recordings.
