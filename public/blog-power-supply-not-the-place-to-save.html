Power supplies: why the PSU is not the place to save money

How many watts a gaming PC needs, what 80 PLUS Gold means, why the exact model number matters, and why every Frame Foundry build ships with the same 850W unit.

5 min read power supply parts buying guide

Short answer: buy a power supply from a known maker, 80 PLUS Gold or better, with a long warranty, and buy it by its exact model number. For the parts in a current gaming PC that means 750W to 850W. We put the same unit in all three of our builds, a Thermaltake Toughpower GF1 850W, and at $76.99 in our price log on Nov 8 it is one of the two cheapest parts in every one of them. A PSU (power supply unit) is the one part that touches everything else, so it is the last place to shave money.

What the power supply does

Everything in the case runs on the power supply's 12 volt output: processor, graphics card, drives, fans. A good unit holds a steady voltage when the graphics card suddenly asks for a few hundred watts more, and shuts itself off before damage is done when something goes wrong. The GF1's spec sheet lists six such protections by name - over current, over voltage, under voltage, over power, short circuit and over temperature. A bad unit runs hot and loud, drops out under load, and when it fails it can take other parts with it.

How many watts you need

Start with the two big consumers, using the makers' own numbers:

TDP (thermal design power) and typical board power are sustained figures, not peaks. Cards and chips pull more for fractions of a second, and the motherboard, memory, drives and fans add a little more. That is why AMD and NVIDIA recommend a unit roughly double what the card itself draws.

We go to 850W in every build for two reasons. Headroom first: a graphics card upgrade in two or three years should not also mean a power supply swap. Noise second: the GF1's fan does not spin at all until the load passes a certain share of its rating, so an 850W unit in a PC drawing 300W to 400W while gaming is silent much of the time. That matters most in the Quiet Creator, where everything else is tuned for low noise too.

What 80 PLUS Gold means

80 PLUS is an independent certification for how much of the power from the wall reaches the parts, with the rest lost as heat. The baseline level requires 80 percent efficiency or better at 10, 20, 50 and 100 percent of rated load, and the higher tiers - Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Titanium - raise that bar. Thermaltake rates the GF1 at up to 90 percent.

Efficiency saves a little on the electric bill, but the bigger win is heat: a unit that wastes less runs cooler, so its fan runs slower and its capacitors last longer. Gold is the sweet spot. The step up from Bronze is real; the step to Platinum or Titanium costs noticeably more for a few extra percent.

Why the exact model number matters

Power supply names are reused across years and regions, and the parts inside change. Two units with the same name on the box can have different platforms, fans, cables and warranties. Shop by the full part number, not the marketing name.

Our own price log shows how easy it is to slip. From Oct 16 to Oct 20 it tracked a "Toughpower GF1 850W" at $139.99 and flagged a price jump. That listing turned out to be the white Snow Edition, EU version. The black US unit we use, part number PS-TPD-0850FNFAGU-P, was $75.90 the whole time, and the log was corrected on Oct 21.

The part number also tells you about cables. The GF1 is fully modular, with six 6+2 pin PCIe connectors on three cables and two 4+4 pin processor cables. The RX 9070 XT takes two 8-pin cables and the RX 9060 XT takes one, straight from the unit. The RTX 5070 Ti is different: NVIDIA's spec page says it takes a single 300W or greater PCIe Gen 5 cable, or two 8-pin cables through the adapter in the card's box. The GF1 has no native Gen 5 cable, so the Quiet Creator uses that adapter, fed by two of the unit's 8-pin cables.

Warranty and the long run

Thermaltake backs the GF1 for 10 years, the longest warranty of any part in our builds. As our warranty page says, parts usually carry one to five years, with power supplies going up to ten, and during our one year labor warranty we handle the manufacturer claim for you. One thing no power supply protects against is what comes in from the wall: our warranty, like every maker's, excludes damage from power surges. A surge protector between the outlet and the PC is cheap insurance.

If your current PC has a no-name unit

Open the side panel and read the label. If the maker is one you have never heard of, there is no 80 PLUS rating, or the unit came bundled with a cheap case, it is a good candidate for a swap - especially before you add a bigger graphics card. Power supply swaps are on our services page: labor is quoted up front before any work starts, and the unit is at cost plus 5% like every part. If the PC is also loud and hot, our post on why a gaming PC gets loud after a year or two covers what else to check on the same visit.
