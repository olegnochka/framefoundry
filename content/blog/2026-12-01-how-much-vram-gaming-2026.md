Short answer: for a new gaming PC in 2026, buy a graphics card with at least 12GB of VRAM (video memory), and 16GB if you play at 1440p or 4K or want the card to last a few years. 8GB still meets the minimum requirements of today's big games, but only at 1080p and low or medium settings. That is why every build on [our builds page](builds.html) uses a 16GB card, including the entry-level Mainstream.

## What VRAM is, and why it runs out

VRAM is the memory soldered onto the graphics card itself. It is separate from the RAM (system memory) on the motherboard, which we covered in [Is 16GB of RAM enough for gaming in 2026?](blog-is-16gb-ram-enough-for-gaming-2026.html). The graphics card keeps the textures, models and lighting data for the scene in front of you in its own memory, because it can read it far faster from there than from anywhere else in the PC.

The amount a game needs goes up with three things: the resolution, the texture quality setting, and ray tracing. When a game wants more than the card has, it has to keep swapping data in from system memory. Depending on the game, you see that as stutter, textures that load in blurry and sharpen late, or the game quietly lowering quality for you. And the memory is part of the card - you cannot add more later.

## What today's games ask for

Two big recent games print the VRAM right in their requirements on Steam:

| Game (Steam page) | Minimum | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Indiana Jones and the Great Circle | RTX 2060 SUPER 8 GB or Radeon RX 6600 8 GB - Low preset, 1080p native, 60 fps target | RTX 3080 Ti 12 GB or Radeon RX 7700 XT 12 GB - High preset, 1440p native, 60 fps target |
| Monster Hunter Wilds | GTX 1660 (6GB) or RX 5500 XT (8GB) - "Lowest" setting, 1080p upscaled from 720p, 30 fps | RTX 2060 Super (8GB) or RX 6600 (8GB) - "Medium" setting, 1080p, 60 fps with Frame Generation |

Read the small print and the pattern is clear. An 8GB card gets you into these games, but the publishers pair it with 1080p and the low to medium presets. The step up to high settings at 1440p in Indiana Jones comes with 12GB cards. Indiana Jones also lists "GPU Hardware Ray Tracing Required" even at minimum, so ray tracing is no longer an optional extra in every game - and ray tracing is one of the things that uses more video memory.

These are the publishers' own targets, not our tests. We do not publish frame rates we have not measured.

## Same card name, two memory sizes

This is the trap when shopping. Several current cards come in two versions that share a name:

- **AMD Radeon RX 9060 XT** - AMD lists two separate products, the RX 9060 XT (8GB) and the RX 9060 XT (16GB). Both use GDDR6 memory on a 128-bit interface; the 16GB one is rated at 160W board power, the 8GB at 150W.
- **NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Ti** - NVIDIA's spec table lists a memory configuration of "16 GB / 8 GB" GDDR7. The plain RTX 5060 below it comes only with 8 GB.

So "RX 9060 XT" or "RTX 5060 Ti" on a prebuilt listing does not tell you the memory size. Look for the number next to it, and if the listing does not say, ask. The 8GB versions are cheaper, but the gap tends to show later, as new games raise their recommended specs.

On Nov 27 the PowerColor Reaper RX 9070 XT 16GB was $1,049.99 and the PNY RTX 5070 Ti 16GB was $1,479.99 (on Oct 14 the log tracked other makers' versions of those two chips, at $879.99 and $1,179.99). Our [fall price post](blog-graphics-card-and-memory-prices-fall-2026.html) has the background.

That makes the 8GB versions tempting, and if you only play esports games at 1080p, an 8GB card can be a reasonable way to save. For everyone else we would rather spend the money once on the memory than replace the card early.

## If you already own an 8GB card

You do not need to panic. Lower the texture quality setting one step first - in most games it is the setting most tied to video memory. Turn ray tracing down or off where the game allows it.
