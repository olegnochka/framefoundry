Short answer: a PC that was quiet when it was new and is loud now has almost always lost cooling, not gotten weaker. Dust blocks the air, the thermal paste between the processor and its cooler dries out, and the fans spin faster to make up for it. Cleaning the dust out and putting fresh paste on the processor brings the temperatures and the noise back down. That is exactly what our $79 [tune-up](services.html) does, at your home, in about an hour. Here is what is going on inside the case.

## Why heat turns into noise

Every PC has a set of rules called fan curves: at this processor or graphics card temperature, spin the fans at this speed. Hotter parts mean faster fans, and faster fans mean more noise. A new PC with a clean cooler sits low on those curves, so you hardly hear it. As cooling gets worse, the same game pushes the same parts to higher temperatures, and the fans climb the curve to keep up. Nothing is broken; the PC is just louder.

There is a hard limit at the top. AMD's spec page for the Ryzen 5 9600X, the processor in our Mainstream and Performance builds, lists a maximum operating temperature of 95°C. A modern processor that reaches its limit slows itself down to stay there, so a hot PC does not fail - it stutters and runs louder, which is how most people first notice.

## 1. Dust

This is the big one. Case fans pull air in through the front and push it out the back and top, and everything in that air - dust, pet hair, carpet fibers - lands on the first surface it meets. On the intake filters it only blocks airflow. On the fins of the processor cooler and the graphics card it also acts like a blanket, so the heat has a harder time leaving the metal.

You can check this without tools. Look at the front of the case with a flashlight, and at the top of the processor cooler through the side panel. Grey fuzz between the fins means the cooler is working through a filter it was never meant to have. Opening the PC to clean dust does not affect our [warranty](warranty.html), and we say so in writing.

## 2. Dried thermal paste

Between the processor and the cooler there is a thin layer of thermal paste that fills the microscopic gaps between the two metal surfaces. Fresh paste is soft and carries heat well. Over years of heating up and cooling down it dries, cracks and pulls away from the edges, and each spot where it has gone is a spot where heat crosses an air gap instead. The symptom is the same as dust: higher temperatures at the same load. The fix is to take the cooler off, clean both surfaces and apply fresh paste, and it is part of every tune-up.

Graphics cards have paste too, but repasting one usually means opening the card, which can affect its maker's warranty. That is why graphics card repasting is not part of the tune-up; it is quoted separately.

## 3. Fan curves that nobody set

Many PCs run on the motherboard's default fan curve, which is chosen to be safe on any cooler, not quiet on yours. A cooler with headroom can run its fans slower at the same temperatures. On our Quiet Creator build this tuning is part of the Premium assembly; on a tune-up we check the curves on whatever PC you have, after the cleaning, because clean and repasted hardware can afford slower fans.

## When it is not dust

If the PC is loud but the temperatures are fine, the noise may be one fan with a worn bearing - a rattle or buzz rather than a rush of air. That is a part swap, not a cleaning. If the PC is clean and cool but still slow, the limit is a part, and our [upgrade or buy new checklist](blog-upgrade-or-buy-new-pc.html) walks through how to find which one.
