Short answer: for a gaming PC with one of today's mainstream processors, a good tower air cooler is the simpler choice, and it is what we put in all three of [our builds](builds.html). Liquid cooling earns its place when the processor runs hot for hours at a time, when the case has no room for a tall cooler, or when you want the look. Here is how the two compare on the three things people actually ask us about: noise, upkeep and price.

## What each one is

An air cooler is a block of metal fins with heat pipes running through it and one or two fans blowing across it. Heat moves from the processor into the pipes, up into the fins, and out into the case air. There are no moving parts except the fans.

A liquid cooler, usually sold as an AIO (all-in-one), is a sealed loop: a small pump sits on the processor, liquid carries the heat through two hoses to a radiator, and fans on the radiator push the heat out of the case. The radiator mounts to the case front or top and comes in sizes named after its fans: 240 mm (two 120 mm fans), 280 mm, 360 mm (three 120 mm fans).

A custom loop, with its own reservoir and hard or soft tubing, is a third category. It is the most expensive and the most work, and it is the one our [services page](services.html) prices separately, quoted per build from $699.

## Noise

Both types make noise the same way: fans spinning. The difference is where the heat ends up and how hard the fans have to work.

A big air cooler has a lot of fin area, so its fans can turn slowly. The be quiet! Dark Rock Pro 5 we use in the Quiet Creator is rated by its maker at 270W, has seven heat pipes and two fans, and lists an overall noise level of 8.9, 16.3 and 23.3 dB(A) at 50, 75 and 100 percent fan speed in its Quiet mode. A switch on the cooler caps the fans at 1500 rpm in that mode or lets them run to 2000 rpm in Performance mode. The Thermalright Peerless Assassin 120 SE in our Mainstream and Performance builds has six heat pipes and two 120 mm fans rated at 1550 rpm and 25.6 dBA maximum, per Thermalright's spec page.

A liquid cooler adds a pump, and the pump runs whenever the PC is on. On the Arctic Liquid Freezer III Pro 360, for example, the spec sheet lists a pump speed of 800 to 2800 rpm under PWM (pulse width modulation) control, plus a small fan on the pump head to cool the motherboard's voltage regulators. The three radiator fans are rated from 600 to 3000 rpm. At idle and light load the pump is the only thing you might hear; under load a radiator can be very quiet because it has so much fan area, but the fans still have to be tuned. That tuning is what the fan-curve work in our [Premium assembly](services.html) is for, and it matters on both kinds of cooler.

## Maintenance and what wears out

An air cooler has nothing to leak, nothing to pump and nothing to refill. Over years its fans can wear, and the thermal paste under it dries out, which is the part of a PC getting louder that our post on [why a gaming PC gets loud after a year or two](blog-why-pc-gets-loud-and-hot.html) covers. A fan is a cheap part to replace, and fresh paste is included in our $79 [tune-up](services.html). be quiet! rates the Dark Rock Pro 5's fans at 300,000 hours and backs the cooler for 3 years.

A sealed liquid cooler has one more thing that can fail: the pump. When a pump stops, the processor heats up within seconds and slows itself down to stay at its limit. AMD lists a maximum operating temperature of 95°C for both the Ryzen 7 9800X3D and the Ryzen 5 9600X, so the PC does not fail, but it will stutter and the fans will roar until the cooler is replaced. The loop also loses a little liquid through the hoses over many years. Makers answer this with long warranties: Arctic's spec sheet for the Liquid Freezer III Pro 360 lists 6 years. The cooler is a sealed unit, so there is no topping up, but when it is done, the whole unit is replaced.

Weight is the quiet difference nobody mentions. The Dark Rock Pro 5 is 1.29 kg hanging off the motherboard; the Peerless Assassin is 730 g. A liquid cooler puts most of its weight in the radiator, bolted to the case. Both arrangements are fine in a PC that stays on a desk, which is why we pack every build with foam inserts for the drive to your door.

## Fit

The first thing to check before buying either is the case. Air coolers are tall: the Peerless Assassin is 155 mm high and the Dark Rock Pro 5 is 168 mm. Every case lists a maximum cooler height; the Lian Li Lancool 207 in our Mainstream and Performance builds lists 180 mm, with room to spare. Liquid coolers trade height for radiator space: the case has to have a mount for a 240, 280 or 360 mm radiator, and a long graphics card can take away the front mount. Tall memory modules can also collide with an air cooler's front fan, which is one of the things we check on the bench.

Liquid cooling is not as expensive as it once was, but a cooler in that class still costs about double what the Peerless Assassin does, and the money buys you a pump and a radiator you may not need.

## Which one for which processor

AMD's own spec pages are a good guide. For the Ryzen 5 9600X, a 65W processor, AMD recommends a "premium air cooler". For the Ryzen 7 9800X3D, a 120W processor, AMD's page says a liquid cooler is recommended for optimal performance. We still chose the Dark Rock Pro 5 for the [Quiet Creator](builds.html): its 270W rating is far above the chip's 120W, and a big air cooler with slow fans fits the build's whole point, which is being quiet in a living room. If you edit video for hours a day or want to push the processor harder, a 360 mm liquid cooler is the upgrade we would price for you, and the North case the Quiet Creator uses takes a front radiator up to 360 mm.
