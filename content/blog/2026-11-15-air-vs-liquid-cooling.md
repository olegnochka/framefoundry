Short answer: for a gaming PC with one of today's mainstream processors, a good tower air cooler is the simpler choice, and it is what we put in all three of [our builds](builds.html). Liquid cooling earns its place when the processor runs hot for hours at a time, when the case has no room for a tall cooler, or when you want the look. Here is how the two compare on noise, upkeep and price.

## What each one is

An air cooler is a block of metal fins with heat pipes running through it and one or two fans blowing across it. Heat moves from the processor into the pipes, up into the fins and out into the case air. The only moving parts are the fans.

A liquid cooler, usually sold as an AIO (all-in-one), is a sealed loop: a pump sits on the processor, liquid carries the heat through two hoses to a radiator, and fans on the radiator push it out of the case. Radiators are named after their fans: 240 mm (two 120 mm fans), 280 mm or 360 mm.

A custom loop with its own reservoir and tubing is a third category, the most expensive and the most work; our [services page](services.html) quotes it per build, from $699.

## Noise

Both types make noise the same way: fans spinning. A big air cooler has a lot of fin area, so its fans can turn slowly. The be quiet! Dark Rock Pro 5 in our Quiet Creator is rated by its maker at 270W, has seven heat pipes and two fans, and lists a noise level of 8.9, 16.3 and 23.3 dB(A) at 50, 75 and 100 percent fan speed in its Quiet mode, where a switch caps the fans at 1500 rpm. The Thermalright Peerless Assassin 120 SE in our Mainstream and Performance builds has six heat pipes and two 120 mm fans rated at 1550 rpm and 25.6 dBA maximum, per Thermalright's spec page.

A liquid cooler adds a pump that runs whenever the PC is on. On the Arctic Liquid Freezer III Pro 360, for example, the spec sheet lists a pump speed of 800 to 2800 rpm, a small fan on the pump head, and three radiator fans rated from 600 to 3000 rpm. At idle the pump is the only thing you might hear. Under load a radiator can be very quiet because it has so much fan area, but the fans still have to be tuned, which is what the fan-curve work in our [Premium assembly](services.html) is for, on either kind of cooler.

## Maintenance and what wears out

An air cooler has nothing to leak, pump or refill. Over years its fans can wear and the thermal paste under it dries out, as our post on [why a gaming PC gets loud after a year or two](blog-why-pc-gets-loud-and-hot.html) explains; fresh paste is part of our $79 [tune-up](services.html). be quiet! rates the Dark Rock Pro 5's fans at 300,000 hours and backs the cooler for 3 years.

A sealed liquid cooler has one more part that can fail: the pump. When a pump stops, the processor heats up within seconds and slows itself down to stay at its limit (AMD lists 95°C for both the Ryzen 7 9800X3D and the Ryzen 5 9600X), so the PC does not break, but it stutters and the fans roar until the cooler is replaced. Makers answer this with long warranties: Arctic's spec sheet for the Liquid Freezer III Pro 360 lists 6 years. There is no topping up a sealed unit; when it is done, the whole thing is swapped.

## Fit

Check the case before buying either. Every case lists a maximum cooler height: the Peerless Assassin is 155 mm tall and the Lian Li Lancool 207 in our Mainstream and Performance builds allows 180 mm. Liquid coolers trade height for radiator space: the case needs a mount for a 240, 280 or 360 mm radiator, and a long graphics card can block the front one.

The Peerless Assassin was $31.41 when the log started on Oct 14 and has sat at $34.90 since Oct 26. The Dark Rock Pro 5 moved between $79.90 and $84.99 through October and has been $79.90 since Nov 1.

For comparison, on Nov 11 PCPartPicker listed the Arctic Liquid Freezer III Pro 360 from $69.89, against the maker's MSRP (manufacturer's suggested retail price) of $99.98. That is about double the Peerless Assassin, and the extra money buys a pump and a radiator you may not need.

## Which one for which processor

AMD's own spec pages are a good guide. For the Ryzen 5 9600X, a 65W processor, AMD recommends a "premium air cooler". For the Ryzen 7 9800X3D, a 120W processor, AMD's page says a liquid cooler is recommended for optimal performance. We still chose the Dark Rock Pro 5 for the [Quiet Creator](builds.html): its 270W rating is far above the chip's 120W, and a big air cooler with slow fans fits the build's whole point, which is being quiet in a living room. If you edit video for hours a day or want to push the processor harder, a 360 mm liquid cooler is the upgrade we would price for you.

If your current PC has a small stock cooler and gets loud in games, a cooler swap is one of the cheapest upgrades there is: labor quoted up front, the part at cost plus 5%.
